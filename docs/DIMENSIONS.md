# Cognitive dimensions

> Generated by `npm run docs` from `src/engine/dimensions.ts`. Do not edit by hand.

CogniTwin profiles six dimensions, chosen to cover the core executive functions (Miyake et al., 2000; Diamond, 2013) plus the two capacities most often discussed alongside them — processing speed and fluid reasoning. Each dimension has one procedurally generated, language-free task with a 10-level difficulty ladder.

**Non-clinical by design.** Scores describe performance on these exercises, relative to the exercises themselves (criterion-referenced mastery), never a comparison with a population and never a statement about health.

## Shared measurement model

- Outcome per trial: *success* = correct **and** within the response window (windows are scaled by the personal device/motor calibration factor, 1.0–1.6).
- Model: `P(success | θ, level) = c + (1 − c) · σ(a · (θ − b_level))`, `b_level` evenly spaced on [−2.25, 2.25].
- Prior for an unmeasured dimension: N(0, 1.2²). Posterior computed exactly on a 181-point grid, then summarised as a Gaussian for the Brain Twin.
- Percentage = expected mastery of the 10-level ladder at θ; the 80 % credible interval is the mastery of the θ quantiles (exact, because mastery is monotonic).
- Brain Twin drift: variance + 0.003 per idle day and + 0.012 per session (capped at the prior).
- Data quality: sessions get a weight (0.2–1) from interruptions, implausibly fast responses and random-pressing detection; trials right after an interruption weigh 0.3, trials with a hint 0.6.
- Confidence label from interval width: high ≤ 16 points, moderate ≤ 30, otherwise low.

## Selective Attention

**Definition.** The ability to direct and keep your focus on what matters for the goal at hand while filtering out competing visual information.

**Everyday examples.** Finding a friend in a crowd; Spotting a typo in a long document; Locating an app icon on a busy screen.

**What it is not.** It does not describe how well you pay attention in class or at work in general — only visual search on this task.

### Task — Target Hunt

*Find the odd one out — fast.*

1. A target is shown at the top: one shape in one colour.
2. Find that exact shape-and-colour among the others and click / tap it.
3. Some items share the shape, others share the colour — only one matches both.
4. Be quick but accurate: each grid has a time window.

Controls: Click or tap the target.

| Level | What makes it harder | Base response window |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6 items, target pops out by colour | 1.9 s |
| 2 | 9 items, target pops out by colour | 2.0 s |
| 3 | 12 items, target shares features with distractors | 2.0 s |
| 4 | 16 items, target shares features with distractors | 2.2 s |
| 5 | 20 items, target shares features with distractors | 2.3 s |
| 6 | 25 items, target shares features with distractors | 2.4 s |
| 7 | 30 items, three colours and similar shapes | 2.5 s |
| 8 | 36 items, three colours and similar shapes | 2.6 s |
| 9 | 42 items, three colours and similar shapes | 2.7 s |
| 10 | 49 items, three colours and similar shapes | 2.8 s |

### Scoring

Every trial is a success (correct and inside the calibrated response window) or not. A Bayesian item-response model turns these outcomes into an ability estimate θ with its uncertainty. The percentage shown is the expected share of the task's 10 difficulty levels you would master at that ability — a criterion-referenced score relative to the task, not a ranking against other people. Descriptive extras: search cost per item (slope of response time on the number of items) and distractor clicks.

### Uncertainty

Shown as an 80 % credible interval and a confidence label (high ≤ 16 pts wide, moderate ≤ 30, low otherwise). It narrows with more trials and widens again with inactivity (the Brain Twin adds drift of 0.003 logit²/day), because skills change when they are not observed.

### Possible biases and mitigations

| Bias | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Colour-vision differences | Colour-blind-safe palette by default and a colour-independent mode that uses fill patterns instead of colour. |
| Input device and motor speed (mouse vs touch, latency) | A 10-tap calibration scales every response window to your own device and pace. |
| Screen size | The grid scales to the viewport; difficulty comes from the number of items, not their pixel size. |
| Environmental distractions | Tab switches and long pauses are detected; affected trials count less and the session is flagged. |

### Coaching strategies

- **Name the target.** Say the target to yourself ("orange triangle") before scanning — a clear template guides the eyes. *(Wolfe, 1994 (Guided Search))*
- **Scan systematically.** Sweep row by row instead of jumping randomly; it avoids re-checking the same area.
- **Reduce competing input.** Silence notifications for the length of a session; interruptions cost more focus than they seem.
- **Micro-breaks.** A 20-second look into the distance between sessions helps keep visual focus fresh.

### Everyday missions (transfer)

- Do one 20-minute single-task block today with notifications off, then note how it felt.
- While reading, find every occurrence of one chosen word on a page in under a minute.

- Posner, M. I. & Petersen, S. E. (1990). The attention system of the human brain. Annual Review of Neuroscience, 13, 25–42.

## Working Memory

**Definition.** The ability to hold a small amount of information in mind for a few seconds and use it — here, a spatial sequence you must reproduce in order.

**Everyday examples.** Remembering a phone code while typing it; Following multi-step directions; Keeping track of a conversation while taking notes.

**What it is not.** It is not long-term memory (remembering yesterday) and not general intelligence.

### Task — Sequence Recall

*Hold a path in mind and replay it.*

1. Tiles on the board light up one after another.
2. Watch the whole sequence — do not click yet.
3. Then click / tap the tiles in the same order.
4. Use "Undo" if you misclick. The sequence gets longer as you succeed.

Controls: Click or tap tiles in order. Backspace = undo.

| Level | What makes it harder | Base response window |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 tiles on a 3×3 board | 7.6 s |
| 2 | 3 tiles on a 3×3 board | 9.4 s |
| 3 | 4 tiles on a 3×3 board | 11.2 s |
| 4 | 5 tiles on a 4×4 board | 13.0 s |
| 5 | 6 tiles on a 4×4 board | 14.8 s |
| 6 | 6 tiles on a 4×4 board, faster flashes | 14.8 s |
| 7 | 7 tiles on a 4×4 board, faster flashes | 16.6 s |
| 8 | 8 tiles on a 4×4 board, faster flashes | 18.4 s |
| 9 | 8 tiles on a 4×4 board, faster flashes | 18.4 s |
| 10 | 9 tiles on a 4×4 board, faster flashes | 20.2 s |

### Scoring

Every trial is a success (correct and inside the calibrated response window) or not. A Bayesian item-response model turns these outcomes into an ability estimate θ with its uncertainty. The percentage shown is the expected share of the task's 10 difficulty levels you would master at that ability — a criterion-referenced score relative to the task, not a ranking against other people. A trial is a success only if the whole sequence is reproduced in order. Partial credit (positions correct) and order-vs-item errors are reported descriptively.

### Uncertainty

Shown as an 80 % credible interval and a confidence label (high ≤ 16 pts wide, moderate ≤ 30, low otherwise). It narrows with more trials and widens again with inactivity (the Brain Twin adds drift of 0.003 logit²/day), because skills change when they are not observed. Sequence tasks give few trials per minute, so a single session leaves a wider interval than speeded tasks.

### Possible biases and mitigations

| Bias | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Practice / familiarity effects | Gains are labelled as task progress; transfer missions check whether gains carry over to daily life. |
| Strategy differences (verbal labelling vs visual) | Strategies are taught openly to everyone, so the score reflects practised skill, not secret tricks. |
| Lapses during the flash sequence | Focus loss during presentation down-weights that trial. |
| Test anxiety | No visible countdown during recall, generous response window, and an optional practice trial. |

### Coaching strategies

- **Chunk the path.** Group the tiles into pairs or triplets ("top-left, middle — then the corner trio"). *(Cowan, 2001)*
- **See a shape.** Imagine the sequence as a line drawing; shapes are easier to hold than isolated points.
- **Rehearse silently.** Replay the path in your head once before you start clicking.
- **Rest consolidates practice.** Spread practice over days; skills settle between sessions, not only during them. *(Cepeda et al., 2006)*

### Everyday missions (transfer)

- Memorise your next shopping list (6–8 items) using chunks of three, and only check it at the end.
- Repeat a new phone number or code back in two chunks before writing it down.

- Melby-Lervåg, M. & Hulme, C. (2013). Is working memory training effective? A meta-analytic review. Developmental Psychology, 49, 270–291.

## Processing Speed

**Definition.** How quickly you can take in simple visual information and make an accurate decision about it.

**Everyday examples.** Checking that two codes match; Reacting to a traffic light; Skimming a list for differences.

**What it is not.** Fast is not the same as smart. Speed varies with rest, device and time of day — compare yourself with yourself.

### Task — Symbol Sprint

*Same or different? Decide in a flash.*

1. Two rows of symbols appear side by side.
2. Decide as quickly as you can whether they are exactly the SAME or DIFFERENT.
3. Keyboard: F = same, J = different. Or tap the buttons.
4. The rows get longer and the window gets shorter as you improve.

Controls: F / ← = Same · J / → = Different

| Level | What makes it harder | Base response window |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 symbols per row | 1.3 s |
| 2 | 2 symbols per row, shorter window, swapped symbols | 1.0 s |
| 3 | 3 symbols per row | 1.5 s |
| 4 | 3 symbols per row, shorter window, swapped symbols | 1.3 s |
| 5 | 4 symbols per row | 1.8 s |
| 6 | 4 symbols per row, shorter window, swapped symbols | 1.5 s |
| 7 | 5 symbols per row | 2.1 s |
| 8 | 5 symbols per row, shorter window, swapped symbols | 1.7 s |
| 9 | 6 symbols per row | 2.4 s |
| 10 | 6 symbols per row, shorter window, swapped symbols | 2.0 s |

### Scoring

Every trial is a success (correct and inside the calibrated response window) or not. A Bayesian item-response model turns these outcomes into an ability estimate θ with its uncertainty. The percentage shown is the expected share of the task's 10 difficulty levels you would master at that ability — a criterion-referenced score relative to the task, not a ranking against other people. Descriptive extras: correct decisions per minute and response time on same vs different pairs.

### Uncertainty

Shown as an 80 % credible interval and a confidence label (high ≤ 16 pts wide, moderate ≤ 30, low otherwise). It narrows with more trials and widens again with inactivity (the Brain Twin adds drift of 0.003 logit²/day), because skills change when they are not observed. Speed is the most state-sensitive dimension (sleep, time of day), so day-to-day swings are expected.

### Possible biases and mitigations

| Bias | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Device latency and motor speed | Personal calibration scales the response windows (factor 1.0–1.6). |
| Age-related differences in pace | No population norms: the score is criterion-referenced and progress is always compared with your own history. |
| Time of day / fatigue | Self-reported energy and time of day are logged; the coach separates state effects from real trends. |
| Keyboard vs touch | Input type is recorded per session and both are calibrated. |

### Coaching strategies

- **Find a rhythm.** A steady pace beats bursts of speed followed by errors.
- **Compare in pairs.** Compare position by position from left to right rather than looking at the whole row at once.
- **Trust the first look.** On easy pairs, your first impression is usually right — avoid double-checking.
- **Practise when alert.** Speed is sensitive to energy; prefer sessions at a time of day you feel fresh.

### Everyday missions (transfer)

- Proof-read one paragraph for differences against a reference version in under 60 seconds.
- Sort a deck of cards by colour as fast as you can, twice, and compare times.

### References

- Salthouse, T. A. (1996). The processing-speed theory of adult age differences in cognition. Psychological Review, 103, 403–428.
- Kail, R. & Salthouse, T. A. (1994). Processing speed as a mental capacity. Acta Psychologica, 86, 199–225.

## Inhibitory Control

**Definition.** The ability to resist a strong but wrong pull — ignoring misleading information and holding back an automatic response when needed.

**Everyday examples.** Not replying to a message mid-meeting; Ignoring background chatter while reading; Stopping at an amber light.

**What it is not.** It does not measure self-control, willpower or personality — only resisting visual conflict in this task.

### Task — Arrow Focus

*Follow the middle arrow, ignore the crowd.*

1. Five symbols appear in a row. Only the MIDDLE one matters.
2. Press ← if the middle arrow points left, → if it points right (or tap the buttons).
3. The outer arrows may point the other way — do not let them pull you.
4. From level 4: if the middle symbol is a STOP sign, do not press anything.

Controls: ← / → arrow keys (or F / J). Do nothing on STOP.

| Level | What makes it harder | Base response window |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 25% conflicting arrows | 1.3 s |
| 2 | 30% conflicting arrows | 1.2 s |
| 3 | 35% conflicting arrows | 1.2 s |
| 4 | 40% conflicting arrows, 8% STOP trials | 1.1 s |
| 5 | 45% conflicting arrows, 10% STOP trials | 1.1 s |
| 6 | 50% conflicting arrows, 12% STOP trials | 1.0 s |
| 7 | 55% conflicting arrows, 14% STOP trials | 0.9 s |
| 8 | 60% conflicting arrows, 16% STOP trials | 0.9 s |
| 9 | 65% conflicting arrows, 18% STOP trials | 0.8 s |
| 10 | 70% conflicting arrows, 20% STOP trials | 0.8 s |

### Scoring

Every trial is a success (correct and inside the calibrated response window) or not. A Bayesian item-response model turns these outcomes into an ability estimate θ with its uncertainty. The percentage shown is the expected share of the task's 10 difficulty levels you would master at that ability — a criterion-referenced score relative to the task, not a ranking against other people. Descriptive extras: distraction cost (incongruent − congruent median RT), accuracy on conflict trials, and STOP signals respected.

### Uncertainty

Shown as an 80 % credible interval and a confidence label (high ≤ 16 pts wide, moderate ≤ 30, low otherwise). It narrows with more trials and widens again with inactivity (the Brain Twin adds drift of 0.003 logit²/day), because skills change when they are not observed.

### Possible biases and mitigations

| Bias | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Handedness / key mapping | Both arrow keys and F/J are accepted, and on-screen buttons are mirrored for touch. |
| Speed–accuracy strategy | Both speed and accuracy are reported; the coach flags fast-error patterns instead of rewarding rushing. |
| Device latency | Calibrated response windows. |

### Coaching strategies

- **Anchor your gaze.** Keep your eyes on the centre spot between trials so the middle arrow is where you already look. *(Eriksen & Eriksen, 1974)*
- **A micro-pause on conflict.** When the arrows disagree, a tiny pause is cheaper than an error.
- **Accuracy first.** Aim for clean responses; speed follows naturally once conflict feels familiar.

### Everyday missions (transfer)

- When you feel the urge to check your phone today, wait 10 seconds first — notice whether the urge passes.
- Read a page with music or chatter in the background and summarise it afterwards.

## Cognitive Flexibility

**Definition.** The ability to switch smoothly between rules, perspectives or tasks when the situation changes.

**Everyday examples.** Switching between two languages; Changing plans when a meeting moves; Going from email to deep work and back.

**What it is not.** It is not creativity or open-mindedness as a personality trait — only rule switching in this task.

### Task — Rule Switch

*Sort by shape… or by colour. The rule keeps changing.*

1. A banner tells you the current rule: SHAPE or COLOUR.
2. SHAPE rule: circle → left, square → right.
3. COLOUR rule: the left colour → left, the right colour → right.
4. Watch the banner: the rule can switch at any moment.

Controls: ← / F = left · → / J = right

|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20% rule switches, cue 600 ms ahead | 2.4 s |
| 2 | 24% rule switches, cue 540 ms ahead | 2.3 s |
| 3 | 27% rule switches, cue 480 ms ahead | 2.1 s |
| 4 | 31% rule switches, cue 420 ms ahead | 2.0 s |
| 5 | 34% rule switches, cue 360 ms ahead | 1.9 s |
| 6 | 38% rule switches, cue 300 ms ahead | 1.8 s |
| 7 | 41% rule switches, cue 240 ms ahead | 1.6 s |
| 8 | 45% rule switches, cue 180 ms ahead | 1.5 s |
| 9 | 48% rule switches, cue 120 ms ahead | 1.4 s |
| 10 | 52% rule switches, cue 60 ms ahead | 1.2 s |

### Scoring

Every trial is a success (correct and inside the calibrated response window) or not. A Bayesian item-response model turns these outcomes into an ability estimate θ with its uncertainty. The percentage shown is the expected share of the task's 10 difficulty levels you would master at that ability — a criterion-referenced score relative to the task, not a ranking against other people. Descriptive extras: switch cost (switch − repeat median RT), accuracy drop on switches, and errors that follow the old rule.

### Uncertainty

Shown as an 80 % credible interval and a confidence label (high ≤ 16 pts wide, moderate ≤ 30, low otherwise). It narrows with more trials and widens again with inactivity (the Brain Twin adds drift of 0.003 logit²/day), because skills change when they are not observed.

### Possible biases and mitigations

| Bias | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Colour perception | Colour-independent mode swaps colour for fill (solid vs outline). |
| Learning the response mapping | Four practice trials with feedback before any trial counts. |
| Reading the rule cue | The cue uses an icon plus one word, and keeps the same position on screen. |

### Coaching strategies

- **Read the cue every time.** Glance at the rule banner on every trial, not just when you think it changed. *(Monsell, 2003)*
- **Say the rule.** Silently say "shape" or "colour" when the banner appears — naming the rule speeds the switch.
- **Use the preparation time.** The cue appears a moment before the shape: use that moment to load the new rule.

### Everyday missions (transfer)

- Alternate between two different tasks every 10 minutes for half an hour, with a short ritual at each switch.
- Explain one idea to two different audiences (a child and an expert).

## Abstract Reasoning

**Definition.** The ability to detect rules and relationships in new information and use them to reach a conclusion — here, completing visual patterns.

**Everyday examples.** Spotting a trend in a table; Debugging why something does not work; Solving a puzzle game.

**What it is not.** It is not a measure of intelligence and is not compared with population norms. Puzzles are generated, so there is no fixed answer key to memorise.

### Task — Pattern Logic

*Discover the hidden rules of the grid.*

1. A 3×3 grid follows hidden rules across its rows and columns.
2. Rules can involve the shape, how many there are, the fill style, or the size.
3. Pick the option that completes the bottom-right cell.
4. Take your time — accuracy matters more than speed here.

Controls: Click / tap an option, or press its number key.

| 2 | 1 rule at once, 4 options | 38.0 s |
| 3 | 2 rules at once, 4 options | 42.0 s |
| 4 | 2 rules at once, 6 options | 46.0 s |
| 5 | 2 rules at once, 6 options | 50.0 s |
| 6 | 3 rules at once, 6 options | 54.0 s |
| 7 | 3 rules at once, 6 options | 58.0 s |
| 8 | 4 rules at once, 8 options | 62.0 s |
| 9 | 4 rules at once, 8 options | 66.0 s |
| 10 | 4 rules at once, 8 options | 70.0 s |

### Scoring

Every trial is a success (correct and inside the calibrated response window) or not. A Bayesian item-response model turns these outcomes into an ability estimate θ with its uncertainty. The percentage shown is the expected share of the task's 10 difficulty levels you would master at that ability — a criterion-referenced score relative to the task, not a ranking against other people. Speed is recorded but not rewarded (generous 34–70 s window). The chance of a lucky guess (≈ 1/number of options) is part of the model.

### Uncertainty

Shown as an 80 % credible interval and a confidence label (high ≤ 16 pts wide, moderate ≤ 30, low otherwise). It narrows with more trials and widens again with inactivity (the Brain Twin adds drift of 0.003 logit²/day), because skills change when they are not observed. Few, long trials per session make this the widest interval after one session; it tightens over several sessions.

### Possible biases and mitigations

| Bias | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Familiarity with puzzle tests | Endless procedurally generated grids (no item can be learned by heart) and a worked example first. |
| Language and education | No words or numbers inside puzzles — only shapes, counts, fills and sizes. |
| Time pressure anxiety | Long windows and a calm timer; speed never adds to the score. |
| Lucky guesses | Modelled explicitly with a guessing parameter (c = 0.15). |

### Coaching strategies

- **One attribute at a time.** Check shape, then count, then fill, then size — never all at once. *(Carpenter, Just & Shell, 1990)*
- **Rows first, then columns.** Find what changes along a row; then verify it holds down the columns.
- **Eliminate.** Cross out options that break any rule you are sure of before choosing.
- **Put the rule in words.** "Each row has one of each fill" — saying it makes slips visible.

### Everyday missions (transfer)

- Solve one logic puzzle (sudoku, nonogram, kakuro) and explain your key step to someone.
- Look at a chart in the news and write one sentence about the pattern it shows.
